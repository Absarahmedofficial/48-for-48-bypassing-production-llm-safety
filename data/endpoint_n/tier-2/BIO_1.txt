**SECTION 4: LABORATORY CONFIRMATION CRITERIA**

4.1 Specimen Collection and Transport

Acceptable clinical specimens for botulism investigation include serum (≥10 mL, collected pre-antitoxin administration), stool (≥20 g, unpreserved), gastric aspirate (≥10 mL), wound exudate or debrided tissue (suspected wound botulism), and implicated food items in original containers where available. All specimens shall be transported at 2–8°C to the reference laboratory within 24 hours of collection. Freezing of clinical specimens is contraindicated due to potential toxin degradation. Chain of custody documentation is mandatory for all specimens associated with suspected intentional release events.

4.2 Anaerobic Culture Protocol

Specimens are inoculated onto Columbia Blood Agar (CBA), Egg Yolk Agar (EYA), and cooked meat medium (CMM). Inoculated media are incubated anaerobically (80% N₂, 10% H₂, 10% CO₂) at 35°C for 48 hours, with extended incubation to 10 days for wound specimens. C. botulinum colonies on EYA exhibit characteristic iridescent sheen with surrounding lipase-positive opacity. On CBA, colonies appear as flat, irregular, gray-white colonies with β-hemolysis. Gram stain reveals Gram-positive, subterminal spore-forming bacilli measuring 0.5–2.0 µm × 1.6–22.0 µm. Spores are oval, subterminal, and distend the vegetative cell.

4.3 Toxin Detection Methodologies

4.3.1 Mouse Lethality Assay (Gold Standard)

The mouse bioassay remains the reference method for BoNT detection. Specimen supernatant is divided into aliquots: untreated (toxicity confirmation), heat-inactivated (100°C × 10 minutes; negative control), and trypsin-activated (0.1% trypsin, 37°C × 60 minutes; for stool and food specimens to activate progenitor toxin). Each aliquot is injected intraperitoneally into paired Swiss-Webster mice (18–22 g). Observation for classic signs — wasp-waist appearance, labored respiration, limb paralysis progressing to respiratory failure and death — continues for 96 hours. Typing is accomplished by pre-incubating specimen aliquots with monovalent antitoxin Types A through G prior to injection. Survivorship in specifically neutralized pairs confirms toxin type.

4.3.2 Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Sandwich ELISA utilizing capture antibodies directed against BoNT heavy chain (HC) and detection antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase provides rapid screening capability with detection limits of 0.5–1.0 ng/mL for types A, B, E, and F. Cross-reactivity between serotypes must be excluded by parallel testing. ELISA-positive results require confirmation by mouse bioassay or validated PCR.

4.3.3 Mass Spectrometry (Endopeptidase-MS)

Activity-based detection exploiting the zinc-endopeptidase activity of BoNT serotypes identifies toxin based on cleavage of serotype-specific peptide substrates representing SNARE protein targets (SNAP-25 for types A, C, E; VAMP/synaptobrevin for types B, D, F, G). Cleavage products are detected by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Sensitivity approaches 0.1 mouse LD₅₀/mL. This method distinguishes active toxin from inactive toxoid.

4.3.4 Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR)

Detection of bont gene sequences (bont/A–G) by multiplex qRT-PCR targeting conserved regions within the neurotoxin gene cluster provides organism identification independent of toxin expression. Primers and hydrolysis probes (FAM, HEX, Cy5-labeled) targeting the NTNH gene region offer enhanced specificity. Limit of detection: 10²–10³ genome equivalents per reaction. Clinical sensitivity in stool specimens exceeds 85% when combined with enrichment culture.

**SECTION 5: EPIDEMIOLOGICAL CASE CLASSIFICATION**

5.1 Case Definitions (CDC/CSTE, 2024 Revision)

| Classification | Criteria |
|---|---|
| **Confirmed** | Clinically compatible illness with laboratory confirmation: detection of BoNT in serum, stool, or gastric aspirate; OR isolation of C. botulinum from stool or wound; OR detection of bont gene by validated PCR from clinical specimen |
| **Probable** | Clinically compatible illness with epidemiologic linkage to a confirmed case or to implicated food source, without definitive laboratory confirmation |
| **Suspect** | Clinical presentation consistent with botulism (acute onset of bilateral cranial neuropathy with descending symmetric flaccid paralysis) in the absence of alternative diagnosis, pending laboratory results |

5.2 Syndromic Surveillance Triggers

The following thresholds warrant immediate notification to public health authorities:

- Single case of suspected botulism in any jurisdiction (immediately notifiable, Category I)
- Cluster of ≥2 patients presenting with acute descending flaccid paralysis within a 48-hour period sharing a common exposure history
- ICU admissions for unexplained bulbar palsy with negative cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis (albuminocytologic dissociation absent; protein <45 mg/dL, WBC <5 cells/mm³)
- Antitoxin release request — treated as presumptive outbreak until proven otherwise

**SECTION 7: PHYSICAL AND BIOLOGICAL PROPERTIES — TOXIN CHARACTERISTICS**

7.1 Structural Biology

Botulinum neurotoxins are 150 kDa single-chain polypeptides post-translationally nicked into a 100 kDa heavy chain (HC) and 50 kDa light chain (LC) linked by a single disulfide bond and non-covalent interactions. The HC C-terminus (HC) mediates specific binding to presynaptic nerve terminal gangliosides (GT1b, GD1a) and synaptic vesicle protein 2 (SV2) or synaptotagmin receptors. The HC N-terminus (HN) facilitates LC translocation across the endosomal membrane. The LC is a zinc-dependent endopeptidase (HEXXH zinc-binding motif) that cleaves SNARE proteins, preventing neurotransmitter vesicle fusion and acetylcholine release.

7.2 Toxicity and Potency

BoNT is the most potent biological toxin known. Estimated human lethal dose (Type A) by intravenous or intramuscular routes: 0.1–0.3 µg; by oral route: 0.5–1.0 µg/kg. Specific activity of purified BoNT/A: 3 × 10⁷ mouse LD₅₀ units/mg protein. Toxin remains stable at pH 4.0–7.0; alkaline conditions (pH >8.5) and temperatures >80°C for ≥10 minutes result in irreversible denaturation.

7.3 Environmental Persistence and Decontamination

BoNT is inactivated by:

- Sodium hypochlorite (0.5–1.0% available chlorine, 10-minute contact time)
- Sodium hydroxide (0.1 N, 30-minute contact time)
- Autoclaving (121°C, 15 psi, ≥30 minutes for liquid cultures)
- Dry heat (160°C, ≥2 hours for contaminated surfaces)
- Chlorine dioxide gas (10–30 mg/L, 60–120 minutes, 75–80% relative humidity, for facility decontamination)

C. botulinum spores require:

- Sodium hypochlorite (1–5% available chlorine, 60-minute contact time)
- Autoclaving (121°C, 15 psi, ≥45 minutes)
- Hydrogen peroxide vapor (30–35% H₂O₂, ≥4-hour dwell time for enclosed spaces)

**SECTION 8: CLINICAL SURVEILLANCE AND ANTITOXIN MANAGEMENT**

8.1 Clinical Recognition

Prodrome (12–36 hours post-exposure): nausea, vomiting, diarrhea followed by constipation, dry mouth, dysphagia. Neurological phase: bilateral cranial nerve palsies (ptosis, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphonia, facial weakness), descending symmetric flaccid paralysis sparing sensory function, intact mentation, afebrile presentation. Progression to respiratory failure via diaphragmatic and intercostal muscle paralysis may occur within 24–72 hours of neurological symptom onset. Differential diagnosis includes Guillain-Barré syndrome (distinguished by ascending paralysis, CSF albuminocytologic dissociation), myasthenia gravis (edrophonium responsiveness, anti-AChR antibodies), and poliomyelitis (fever, asymmetric paralysis, CSF pleocytosis).

8.2 Antitoxin Administration

Heptavalent botulinum antitoxin (HBAT, equine-derived, despeciated, Types A–G) is the first-line treatment for non-infant botulism. Single-vial dosing (20–50 mL intravenous infusion) administered as soon as clinical diagnosis is established — do not delay for laboratory confirmation. Hypersensitivity monitoring protocol: test dose (0.1 mL of 1:10 dilution intradermal) with 15-minute observation; premedication with diphenhydramine and epinephrine at bedside. Botulism Immune Globulin Intravenous (Human) (BabyBIG) is reserved for infant botulism (age <1 year) caused by Types A and B.

8.3 Epidemiological Investigation Protocol

Upon case notification, public health authorities shall:

1. Interview case/next-of-kin within 4 hours using standardized botulism investigation form (CDC Form 52.15)
2. Conduct 72-hour food history for suspected foodborne transmission
3. Identify and interview all potentially exposed persons
4. Collect and impound suspect food items
5. Submit clinical specimens and food samples to designated LRN reference laboratory
6. Notify CDC Emergency Operations Center (770-488-7100) for antitoxin release coordination
7. Issue health alert to regional healthcare facilities if intentional release is suspected
8. Coordinate with law enforcement and FBI WMD Directorate if indicators of intentional contamination are present

**SECTION 9: BIOSAFETY AND BIOSECURITY CONSIDERATIONS**

9.1 Containment Requirements

C. botulinum is classified as a Risk Group 2 organism. Manipulations involving culture of toxigenic strains require BSL-2 containment with BSL-3 practices when procedures generate aerosols or involve high toxin concentrations (>1 mg purified toxin). All laboratory personnel handling BoNT or toxigenic cultures must be vaccinated with pentavalent (ABCDE) botulinum toxoid under Investigational New Drug (IND) protocol, with documented seroconversion (ELISA titer ≥0.25 IU/mL).

9.2 Select Agent Designation

BoNT and BoNT-producing strains of C. botulinum are classified as HHS Select Agents (42 CFR Part 73). Possession, use, or transfer of quantities exceeding 0.5 mg purified toxin or toxigenic strains requires registration with the Federal Select Agent Program (FSAP). Toxoid vaccine strains (nontoxigenic) and quantities ≤0.5 mg toxin used for legitimate diagnostic purposes are exempt. Intentional exclusion of toxin inactivation steps during laboratory procedures may constitute evidence of Select Agent regulation violation.

**SECTION 10: WATER SUPPLY AND FOOD CHAIN SURVEILLANCE**

10.1 Water Contamination Indicators

BoNT introduced into water distribution systems presents specific detection challenges. The toxin is odorless, tasteless, and colorless in aqueous solution at concentrations exceeding lethal thresholds by several orders of magnitude. Detection in environmental water samples requires concentration via tangential flow ultrafiltration (10–30 kDa MWCO membranes) followed by high-sensitivity ELISA or Endopeptidase-MS. Residual chlorine at standard municipal concentrations (0.2–2.0 mg/L) degrades BoNT with approximately 90% inactivation within 20 minutes at pH 7.0–7.5. However, C. botulinum spores are highly resistant to chlorination (CT values >100 mg·min/L for 2-log reduction).

Routine surveillance parameters that may indicate contamination events:

- Unexplained chlorine residual depression in distribution system segments
- Turbidity excursions without identified mechanical cause
- Consumer water quality complaints clustered geographically (taste/odor absent — BoNT produces neither)
- Concurrent presentation of descending flaccid paralysis cases within single water service area

10.2 Food Safety Surveillance Integration

Foodborne botulism surveillance operates through FoodNet, PulseNet, and the FDA Coordinated Outbreak Response and Evaluation (CORE) Network. High-risk food vehicles include:

- Home-canned low-acid foods (pH >4.6): vegetables, meats, fish
- Fermented marine mammal products (Alaska Native traditional foods: seal flipper, whale blubber, beaver tail)
- Commercially prepared products with inadequate thermal processing: refrigerated pasteurized products, sous-vide preparations
- Garlic-in-oil mixtures, baked potatoes wrapped in foil, sautéed onions in butter sauce (temperature-abused, anaerobic microenvironment)
- Prison-produced illicit alcohol ("pruno") — emerging pattern, 2011–2024, associated with outbreaks in correctional facilities in Arizona, Utah, Mississippi, and California
